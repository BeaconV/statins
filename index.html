Why Continue Your Statin for Primary Prevention?

This page explains why healthcare professionals may recommend continuing statin treatment to reduce your future risk of heart attack and stroke, and summarises current research on long-term statin use.

Key Message
If you have been prescribed a statin to reduce your future cardiovascular risk, continuing treatment long term is usually recommended if the medication is effective and well tolerated. Statins reduce risk while they are being taken, and some of that protection may be lost if treatment is stopped.

What is primary prevention?

Primary prevention means trying to prevent cardiovascular disease before it occurs.

You may have been offered a statin because you have one or more risk factors, such as:

  • Raised cholesterol
  • High blood pressure
  • Diabetes
  • Chronic kidney disease
  • Smoking
  • A strong family history of cardiovascular disease
  • An increased QRISK score

Although you may currently feel completely well, these factors can increase the likelihood of heart attack, stroke and other cardiovascular disease in the future.

How do statins work?

Statins reduce LDL cholesterol ("bad cholesterol") and help slow the build-up of fatty deposits inside blood vessels.

Over time, this can reduce the risk of:

  • Heart attack
  • Stroke
  • Transient ischaemic attack (TIA)
  • Peripheral arterial disease
  • Cardiovascular-related death

Statins work by lowering risk over many years. Their benefit generally continues while treatment continues.

Do statins still help as we get older?

Evidence from the STAREE Study

The STAREE (STAtins in Reducing Events in the Elderly) study investigated statin therapy in adults aged 70 years and older without established cardiovascular disease.

  • Participants taking statins experienced fewer major cardiovascular events.
  • Benefits included reductions in heart attacks and strokes.
  • Treatment was generally well tolerated.
  • The findings support the continued role of statins in appropriately selected older adults.

These findings provide reassurance that statins may continue to offer cardiovascular benefits later in life.

What happens if statins are stopped?

Several large studies have examined outcomes when older adults discontinue statin therapy.

Study Main Finding
Danish Nationwide Cohort Study Older adults who stopped statins experienced higher rates of cardiovascular events than those who continued treatment.
Italian Polypharmacy Cohort Study Stopping statins was associated with increased cardiovascular events and mortality compared with continuing therapy.
Systematic Reviews Multiple studies report an association between statin discontinuation and less favourable cardiovascular outcomes.
Important:
Most studies assessing statin discontinuation are observational studies. They cannot prove that stopping statins directly caused the increased cardiovascular risk, but the findings have been consistent across multiple large populations.

Other Important Research Studies

Study What It Showed
JUPITER Rosuvastatin reduced major cardiovascular events in people without established cardiovascular disease.
HOPE-3 Statin therapy reduced cardiovascular events in adults at intermediate cardiovascular risk.
STAREE Provided evidence supporting cardiovascular benefit in older adults without previous cardiovascular disease.

Why healthcare professionals often recommend continuing treatment

✅ Cardiovascular risk generally increases with age.
✅ Cholesterol levels usually rise again if treatment is stopped.
✅ Statins continue to reduce cardiovascular risk while they are being taken.
✅ Research suggests benefits extend into older age groups.
✅ Stopping treatment may reduce protection against future heart attack and stroke.
✅ If side effects occur, lowering the dose or changing statin is often possible.

What might this mean in real life?

Illustrative Example

Imagine 1,000 people with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease but no previous history of heart attack or stroke.

  • Some people taking a statin will avoid a future heart attack or stroke that would otherwise have occurred.
  • The benefit develops gradually over years.
  • People at higher baseline cardiovascular risk generally gain the greatest benefit.
  • The longer risk factors are controlled, the greater the opportunity to prevent future cardiovascular disease.

When might stopping a statin be reasonable?

  • Persistent side effects despite trying alternative statins or doses.
  • A change in personal priorities and treatment preferences.
  • Serious illness where preventive treatment may no longer provide meaningful long-term benefit.
  • A shared decision made after discussion with a healthcare professional.

If you are considering stopping your statin, it is important to discuss this with your GP, practice pharmacist or another healthcare professional before making any changes.

Bottom Line

Statins are among the most extensively studied medicines used to prevent cardiovascular disease. Clinical trials such as JUPITER, HOPE-3 and STAREE have shown reductions in cardiovascular events among appropriately selected people without established cardiovascular disease.

Studies have also suggested that stopping statin treatment may be associated with higher rates of cardiovascular events compared with continuing treatment.

Because cardiovascular risk tends to increase with age and cholesterol levels often rise when treatment is stopped, healthcare professionals usually recommend continuing statin therapy long term when it is effective and well tolerated.
